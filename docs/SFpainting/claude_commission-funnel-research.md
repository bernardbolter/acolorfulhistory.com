# 1. What the audit got wrong

The Sept 12 audit put `/available` — a specification grid of existing work — at
the top of the build order, on the reasoning that agents want work they can have
this week.

With no inventory that route has nothing to show, and building a shop window for
an empty shop is worse than not building it. **Scratch `/available` from the
build order.** The filter work in §4.4 of that document still stands on its own
merits for the browse experience; it is just no longer the commercial priority.

Everything below replaces §4.1–4.2 of the audit.

# 2. What's out there

Five sources worth learning from, in descending order of usefulness.

## 2.1 Angela Simeone — the gold standard, and the long-page model

The most complete artist trade program I found. Concrete terms, stated plainly:

| | |
|---|---|
| Trade discount | **20% off retail** for verified designers and design firms, on originals *and* commissions |
| Commission lead time | **~4 weeks from deposit to delivery**, "depending on current studio schedule" |
| Application | **None.** "Send a brief introduction from your studio email along with your firm name and website" → receive a trade code |
| Payment | **In full at order**, for both inventory and commissions |
| Page length | ~2,500–3,000 words, heavy imagery |

Also on the page: ready-to-ship and commissions presented as two clearly
separated options, designer presentation materials, a project-types section with
named commercial credits, a getting-started sequence, and an FAQ.

Credibility carried by press (*Architectural Digest*, *Luxe*, *Forbes*,
*Domino*, *House Beautiful*), named installations (Conrad Nashville, Grand Hyatt
Nashville, Sarah Cannon Cancer Center), 20+ years, and a West Elm / Minted
licensing partnership.

**Two things worth stealing outright:**

1. **No application gate.** A trade code issued off one email from a studio
   address. Every extra field between a designer and a price is a designer who
   closes the tab.
2. **Numbers on the page.** Discount, lead time, and payment terms stated in
   public. This is the single biggest difference between her page and every
   vaguer one I looked at.

## 2.2 Melanie Biehle — the short-page model

The opposite approach and instructive for exactly that reason. ~350–400 words.
Three routes in (licensed print reproductions through Four Hands, custom
wallcoverings through Look Walls, direct studio sourcing of originals). **No
discount disclosed** — deferred to a form-based application which the artist
reviews before sending onboarding materials. Commission detail lives on a
separate page.

## 2.3 The lesson from those two together

**Short page + application gate, or long page + no gate. Not both, and not
neither.**

A short page that also withholds the terms gives the designer nothing to decide
on. A long page that *also* makes them apply is asking twice.

For a practice with no inventory and where every commission is a conversation
anyway, **short page + light qualification** is the right half of that trade —
and it is what you asked for. But it means the short page has to be honest about
price and timeline, or it is just a contact form with a nice photograph.

## 2.4 Uprise Art — designers are buying logistics, not only art

The advisory model, reduced to three named steps: **Meet** (call or email to
discuss the project) → **Artwork selection** (a curated set at trade pricing) →
**Details** (framing, delivery and installation coordinated to the designer's
timeline).

The takeaway is the third step. A designer is not only choosing a painting; they
are offloading a problem. Whoever handles framing, delivery, timing and
installation is the one who gets used again. For a one-man practice this is
actually an advantage — you *can* hand-deliver and hang it, which no marketplace
can.

## 2.5 The house-portrait market — the cautionary comparable

There is an entire commodity industry selling "custom house portrait, realtor
closing gift" — PortraitFlip, From Pic To Art, Give A Masterpiece, Welham & Co,
and hundreds of Etsy sellers. Price points in the low hundreds. Turnaround in
days. Photo-to-painting, often filtered rather than painted.

**This is what a real estate agent finds when they google your product
category.** The neighborhood commission is one search away from a $150 Etsy
house portrait, and nothing on a page protects you from that comparison except
being visibly, immediately a different thing.

Three separations to make explicit in the copy:

- **It is not their house.** It is the neighborhood's history — a photograph
  from before the client was born, of the street they now live on. The house
  portrait market cannot do this; it requires an archive and research.
- **There is a record.** Juried selection, an auction resale, a prior designer
  commission, thirty years of work, and a permanent public archive. The Etsy
  seller has a shop rating.
- **It is a painting.** Acrylic photo transfer on canvas, made by hand in a
  studio, not a filter applied to a submitted JPEG.

This also sharpens the agent path — see §4.2.

## 2.6 Commission mechanics — what the practitioners actually recommend

From the RedDotBlog guide to commission agreements, which is the most practical
writing I found on this:

- **50% deposit, 50% on completion**, final payment before delivery or shipment.
- **State plainly that there is no step-by-step approval process.** Either no
  sketches and no in-progress photos at all, or one preliminary sketch with up
  to two minor adjustments and nothing once the final work is underway.
- **Non-refundable deposit**, stated.
- **Artist retains copyright and reproduction rights.**
- **An email summary is the agreement.** "A clear email summary works
  beautifully in most cases" — the agreement is a communication tool that builds
  confidence, not a legal instrument.

That last point kills a build. You do not need contract infrastructure, e-sign,
or a legal flow. You need a well-written confirmation email and a page that says
what is in it.

## 2.7 Tear sheets — the artefact designers actually work with

Designers assemble client presentations from tear sheets: one-page PDFs with
image, dimensions, medium, price and a line about the maker. Studio Designer —
the project-management tool much of the trade runs on — has tear sheet creation
built in, and ArtCloud generates them on the gallery side.

This matters more for you than for most artists, for a specific reason. See §3.

# 3. The reframe

## An inventory page sells selection. A commission page sells a painting that does not exist yet.

That is the whole difference, and it changes what the page is for.

When there is inventory, the visitor's question is *which one*, and the page's
job is filtering, comparison, and a good photograph. When there is no inventory,
the visitor's question is *what am I going to get*, and the page's job is to make
an imagined painting feel safe to commit to.

Three fears, in order. A commission page that answers all three can be very
short. One that answers none can be very long and still fail.

| Fear | What answers it |
|---|---|
| **What will I actually get?** | The source photograph, the size to scale, the palette, prior work in the same mode |
| **When?** | A stated lead time, and honesty about the SF trip window |
| **What if it's wrong?** | The sketch/approval policy, the deposit terms, and thirty years of evidence that you finish things |

## Which makes the tear sheet your highest-leverage asset

A designer cannot sell your commission to their client by forwarding a URL. They
need a page they can drop into a presentation, next to the rug and the light
fixture.

And you are unusually well placed to generate one, because the hard part of a
commission tear sheet — showing something that does not exist — is a problem you
have already solved twice over. You have the source photographs, the archive
record, `overlayColors` per work, and `compositor.html` for scale composition.

A one-page PDF carrying:

```
[ source photograph ]     the place, the year, the archive it came from
[ scale diagram ]         36×36in against a 9ft wall, human figure for scale
[ palette ]               three swatches, from a comparable finished work
[ two finished works ]    same technique, so the client can see the hand
Neighborhood · Size · Medium · Lead time · Price · Artist, one line
```

That is the deliverable a designer forwards. It is worth more than any page
section, and it is a small build on top of things that already exist.

**Not for the first version.** But it is the thing to aim the funnel at, and
worth knowing now so the page and the intake are shaped to produce it.

# 4. The two paths, sharpened

## 4.1 Interior designers — the real business

Everything in the research points the same way: designers are repeat buyers, they
work to pre-approved budgets, they choose on **scale and colour**, and they value
logistical reliability as much as the work. One good project becomes three.

What they need from the page: trade terms, lead time, working sizes, something
to show a client, and evidence you are reliable. Not the artist statement.

## 4.2 Real estate agents — narrow it to luxury, or it doesn't work

This is a correction to the earlier audit, and to the Aug 25 brief.

At $1,800 a painting, the general agent market is not a market. That buyer is
being served — badly, but served — by the $150 house-portrait industry, and a
typical closing gift budget is a hundred dollars or two.

The arithmetic only works at the top. On a $2.5M San Francisco sale, a 2.5%
side is roughly $62,500, and an $1,800 gift is under 3% of it — a rounding error
against a referral relationship. Those agents already give meaningful gifts, and
they compete on being memorable.

So the agent path is not "real estate agents." It is **luxury SF agents, and the
gift is of the neighborhood, not the house.** Everything else about the page
follows: price stays visible, the Etsy comparison never gets made, and the offer
is scarce rather than cheap.

Worth saying plainly: this path is the smaller one. It is worth a clear section
and no more. **The designer path is the business.**

# 5. Proposed page

## 5.1 Routing

Keep what is built. Add a front door.

| Route | Role | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **`/commissions`** NEW | The clean landing page. Both paths. The front door you send to a designer or hand out on a studio visit. | **~500–700 words** |
| `/neighborhood` existing | The San Francisco series in depth — the Berlin/SF frame, the three tiers, the browse-photos set. One click down, for people who want the story. | unchanged |

Nothing built is wasted. The long page becomes the supporting page it should
always have been, and the short page does the work of converting.

## 5.2 Structure — six blocks, nothing else

```
1  HERO
   One line. One image — a finished painting, not a collage.
   "Commission a painting of a place that means something."
   Sub: neighborhood history, painted by hand in San Francisco,
        delivered by hand.

2  THE FORK — two cards, side by side, equal weight
   ┌────────────────────────┐  ┌────────────────────────┐
   │ For interior designers │  │ For real estate agents  │
   │ Trade pricing          │  │ One place, one painting │
   │ 8–12 weeks             │  │ For a client who just   │
   │ Presentation materials │  │ bought the neighborhood │
   └────────────────────────┘  └────────────────────────┘

3  HOW IT WORKS — three steps, numbered. Not four.
   1  Tell me the place.
   2  I find the photograph and send a proposal — image, size
      to scale, price, delivery date.
   3  Half to start. Painted in the studio. Balance on delivery.

4  WHAT YOU GET — one row of facts, no prose
   36 × 36 in · acrylic photo transfer on canvas · 8–12 weeks ·
   from $1,800 · hand-delivered in San Francisco ·
   permanently documented in the archive

5  PROOF — three lines. Lines, not paragraphs.
   · Selected, ArtSpan Selections 2017 — juried. Later resold at auction.
   · Commissioned for a private home through a San Francisco designer.
   · Thirty years of work, permanently archived →

6  THE FORM — six fields, one screen
   Name · Email · I am a [designer / agent / homeowner] ·
   The place · Rough size · When you need it
```

Trade specifics — discount, deposit, invoicing, W-9, delivery radius — go in a
disclosure block under the designer card, or on `/commissions/trade`. Agents do
not need them and designers will look for them.

## 5.3 What comes off the page

These are all good, and all belong on `/neighborhood` instead:

- **The Berlin / San Francisco honest frame.** A lovely paragraph that does real
  trust work for a bespoke commission — but it is an *explanation*, and
  explanations belong one level down. A designer with a wall does not need your
  biography before your price.
- **"Founding collectors — first five commissions."** Collector framing. To a
  designer it reads as either a gimmick or an admission of inexperience, and it
  actively conflicts with trade pricing (§6.1).
- **The three tiers.** Browse / revisited / fully custom is a good structure for
  someone reading carefully. On a landing page it is a decision you are asking
  for before the visitor has decided the bigger thing. Fold it into step 1 —
  "tell me the place" covers all three, and you can route them in the reply.

## 5.4 One copy note

**"from $1,800"**, not "$1,800." A designer needs the floor to know whether you
are in range; a single number reads as a fixed rate and leaves no room for the
larger and trade-referred work you actually want to be doing.

# 6. Decisions before this becomes a brief

## 6.1 The pricing conflict — this one blocks the page

You currently have a **founding price** ($1,800, first five, framed as
deliberately low) and a plan for **designer-referred pricing** ($2,500–3,000+).
Those are two different numbers for the same painting, and a trade discount
cannot sit on top of either without breaking something:

- 20% off $1,800 is $1,440 — below a price you have already called
  intentionally low, and well into the territory where a serious buyer reads it
  as *something must be wrong with this*.
- Quoting a designer $2,500 while the public page says $1,800 is the fastest way
  to lose a designer permanently. They will see the page.

Three coherent ways out:

| Option | Public page says | Designer gets | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A — Retail up, discount real** | from $2,500 | 20% → $2,000 | Matches trade convention. Founding price becomes a private, direct-only offer, off the page entirely. |
| **B — One price, no discount** | from $1,800 | $1,800, plus designer-only sizes and terms | Honest and simple. Some designers will decline on principle — the discount is how they make margin. |
| **C — Size ladder** | from $1,800 (36in) · from $3,200 (48in) | 20% on the ladder | Discount lives on the larger work designers specify anyway. Probably the best of the three. |

I lean **C**. It resolves the conflict without ever discounting the entry price,
and large-scale statement work is what designers are looking for in the first
place. But this is a business decision, not a design one, and it is yours.

## 6.2 The rest

2. **Trade discount — yes or no, and at what number?** 20% is the convention.
   Tied to 6.1.
3. **Sketch policy.** None at all, or one sketch plus two minor adjustments and
   nothing after the work is underway? Affects lead time and the copy in step 2.
4. **Deposit.** 50/50 is the practitioner standard; Simeone takes payment in
   full at order. 50/50 is the safer position for a first trade relationship.
5. **Lead time — what is the real number?** Simeone quotes four weeks. Yours is
   constrained by SF trips, so the honest answer is probably "8–12 weeks,
   scheduled around studio visits to San Francisco" — and being explicit about
   the trip cadence is a feature, not an apology. What is the actual cadence?
6. **Delivery radius.** Hand-delivery in SF is a real differentiator and a real
   constraint. Do you ship outside the Bay Area, and does a Berlin commission
   exist as a product at all?
7. **Do you want the wireframe next?** I can comp the page — desktop and mobile,
   both paths — before anything is specced, per the usual order. Say the word and
   I'll build it as something you can push around rather than a static picture.
